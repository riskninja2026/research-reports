THE THIRTY-SECOND VERSION

If you read only one screen, read this one

  • Who NVIDIA is: the designer of the chips and systems that train and run AI. It owns no factories — contract manufacturers build the chips — and its CUDA software keeps customers on the platform. Quarterly revenue hit US$96.2bn, more than double a year ago, and about 62 cents of every revenue dollar became net profit.
  • The new twist: NVIDIA increasingly helps finance its own demand. Big customers get up to a year to pay, NVIDIA buys cloud capacity and equity stakes in its ecosystem, and it has promised guarantees that can reach US$108.5bn. Demand is real — but NVIDIA now shares its customers' credit risk.
  • Earnings caution: reported profit is flattered by US$7.8bn of investment gains this quarter, and only 40% of reported profit arrived as operating cash — customers paying later, a big inventory build ahead of the new Rubin chips, and tax timing absorbed the rest.
  • China: the next-quarter outlook assumes zero China Data Center compute revenue. Any recovery is upside, but being shut out also hands local rivals room to grow into global competitors.
  • The verdict: constructive, with a higher risk label than a pure chip story deserves. The base-case value is US$254–321 per share against an after-hours price of US$219.53; the bear case is US$106–148. The price sits below base value — but the bear case is a long way down.
PRIMER · THREE MINUTES ON THE BUSINESS

A chip designer that became the arms dealer of AI

NVIDIA designs GPUs — processors built to do huge numbers of calculations in parallel, which is exactly what training and running AI models requires. It is a fabless company: it designs the chips and systems, and contract manufacturers fabricate them. Its customers are mostly the giant cloud companies (hyperscalers), AI-cloud providers, enterprises and governments building AI computing capacity.

STEP 1
Design the platform

Chips, networking and whole systems, refreshed on a yearly cadence — Blackwell today, Vera Rubin next.

STEP 2
Sell to the AI builders

Cloud giants and AI companies buy systems by the tens of billions. One direct customer alone was 16% of quarterly revenue.

STEP 3
CUDA keeps them

The software layer NVIDIA chips are programmed through. Leaving means rebuilding performance and reliability elsewhere — costly, so customers stay.

STEP 4
Now: finance the buyers

The new step this report scrutinizes — longer payment terms, equity stakes, cloud purchases and guarantees that support customers' own build-outs.

Three reading aids before the tables. First, the calendar: NVIDIA's fiscal year runs ahead of the calendar — "FY2027" is roughly calendar 2026, and this report covers the quarter ended 26 July 2026. Second, the product map: "Data Center" — the AI-chip business — is 92.5% of revenue; the gaming graphics business that made NVIDIA famous is now a sideline. Third, the numbers to trust: figures here come from SEC filings; the earnings call and broker research supply context only. Terms with a dotted underline are defined in the glossary near the end.

Why this quarter matters: the AI build-out is entering its capital-heavy phase. Some of NVIDIA's biggest customers are spending more on data centers than their businesses generate in cash, and NVIDIA itself has started standing behind some of that spending. The central question of this report is not "is AI demand real?" — it clearly is — but "how much of NVIDIA's future demand depends on NVIDIA's own balance sheet, and what is that risk worth?"

PLAIN ENGLISH

The scale is hard to overstate: this single quarter's revenue (US$96.2bn) is more than three and a half times NVIDIA's entire FY2022 year, and the rolling twelve months (US$303.0bn) is about eleven times that year. Margins are the kind normally seen in software, not hardware: 75 cents of gross profit per revenue dollar, 62 cents of net profit.

The one blemish sits in the cash column. Over full years, NVIDIA converts profit to cash beautifully. But this quarter operating cash flow (US$24.1bn) was only about 40% of reported net income (US$59.7bn). Two reasons, detailed later: customers are being allowed to pay later, and inventory is being stockpiled ahead of the Rubin launch. Neither is alarming by itself — but both move risk onto NVIDIA's own books, which is the thread the rest of this report pulls.

PRODUCT · R&D · CUDA

Rubin is early; the lifecycle stack is the moat

The near-term test is the handoff from the current Blackwell chips to the next generation, Vera Rubin. The filings do not reveal product-level yields or margins, so investors can only watch for the absence of bad news: the transition needs to happen without another inventory write-down or a deeper drop in the group's gross margin.

R&D is expensed; infrastructure is the fastest-growing input

NVIDIA spent US$7.054 billion on R&D this quarter, up 64% from a year ago. Within that, spending on computing infrastructure — the machines NVIDIA uses to develop its own products — grew 127%, and pay grew 30%. All of this is expensed as it happens rather than parked on the balance sheet, so the profit numbers already absorb it. What the filings cannot tell you is the return on any particular architecture. The practical test: does each yearly product generation raise customers' useful output faster than memory, power and software costs rise?

People and patents back the machine: at FY2026 year-end, 31,000 of roughly 42,000 employees — about three-quarters — worked in R&D. NVIDIA holds numerous patents expiring between March 2026 and June 2045, but discloses no count or per-patent revenue; protection rests on patents, trade secrets and licenses together. The recent non-exclusive Groq technology license is a live example of return risk: its US$2.5 billion developed-technology asset still has to be turned into commercial products, and the larger goodwill piece is an accounting entry, not proof of a moat.

PLAIN ENGLISH

CUDA is not just a programming interface. The hard-to-copy layer is everything around it in production — the kernels, inference engines, memory management, routing and system integration that make AI hardware actually deliver. A rival can let you port your code; reproducing the performance and reliability is the expensive part. But this advantage is re-earned workload by workload — custom chips built by the cloud giants and rival merchant silicon keep testing it.

Memory moves the margin trough

Management told investors on the call that gross margin should bottom at 71%–72% in Q4 FY2027 and settle at 72%–73% in FY2028 after price increases. The pressure is real: Samsung and SK hynix both describe tight server-memory supply and the commercial ramp of HBM4, the high-bandwidth memory NVIDIA's systems depend on. Goldman reads the promised floor as a possible end to the market's memory-cost fears — but a floor promised is not a floor delivered. The claim fails if margin breaks below the stated trough, or if the price increases themselves start to dent demand.

INDUSTRY · CHINA · OVERSEAS

Demand is strong, but the buyers are financing it

The biggest customers' own filings back up NVIDIA's demand story: cloud revenue is accelerating and AI infrastructure spending is enormous. The same filings show the strain. Free cash flow is falling at some buyers as they borrow and raise capital to build, and customer-designed accelerator chips remain a workload-specific alternative. Booming demand and stretched buyers can be true at the same time — that combination is precisely why NVIDIA's new financing role matters.

China is excluded from the base case

China has effectively dropped out of the numbers. Licensed H200 shipments were under 1% of quarterly Data Center revenue; the first half absorbed a US$0.4 billion charge for H200 inventory and purchase obligations; licensed units must pass a U.S. inspection and carry a 25% tariff NVIDIA says it cannot pass on. The next-quarter outlook assumes zero China Data Center compute revenue.

The damage is not just lost sales. NVIDIA itself warns that being locked out helps rival hardware and developer ecosystems grow — including globally relevant open AI models running on competing platforms. So China stays out of the base case as revenue, remains possible upside if licensing improves, and meanwhile incubates future competition.

Reported geography is a billing map, not consumption

Customers headquartered outside the United States were 38% of Q2 revenue — but read that carefully. NVIDIA warns that where a direct customer is headquartered can differ from where products ship or who ultimately uses them. Concentration has the same blind spot: one direct customer was 16% of Q2 revenue, while a separately described AI research company may be buying through several direct and indirect channels at once, so true end-customer concentration is unknowable from the filing.

Currency, unusually, is a non-issue: essentially all sales are billed in U.S. dollars, hedging covers mainly operating expenses, and the related first-half amounts were not significant. Nearly all cash held abroad can come home without extra federal tax (about US$1.7 billion could incur foreign or state tax). The overseas risks that actually matter are export licensing and the offshore manufacturing chain, not exchange rates.

PLAIN ENGLISH

Follow the money in the buyer table: Alphabet's cloud is growing 82% but its free cash flow went negative; Amazon's trailing-year free cash flow is negative too; Microsoft added US$115.9bn of plant in a year; Meta plans up to US$145bn of 2026 capex (capital spending). Free cash flow is falling at some of these buyers, and debt and equity funding are rising. That doesn't make the demand fake — it means part of the demand is financed, and financed demand is more fragile in a downturn.

EARNINGS QUALITY · NON-GAAP

Operating leverage is real; GAAP income overstates the run rate

The core machine got stronger this quarter — but the reported bottom line got extra help from below the operating line. Gains on equity investments equaled 10.87% of quarterly pretax income; across the first half they reached US$23.707 billion, or 16.76% of pretax income. Those gains come from NVIDIA's investment portfolio riding the AI boom, not from selling chips, and they can reverse.

This distinction between what the chip business earns and what the portfolio earned this quarter runs through the valuation section below.

CASH · CREDIT · CALL

NVIDIA is becoming a credit allocator

Here is the report's core concern in one sentence: even as profits soared, less of them arrived as cash, because NVIDIA is extending credit to customers and building inventory. Accounts receivable — money customers owe but haven't paid — grew faster than revenue after selected investment-grade customers were given terms of 90 days up to a year for large multi-quarter builds. Inventory rose ahead of the Rubin launch. Neither proves demand is weak; both shift timing and counterparty risk onto NVIDIA.

The largest single piece deserves its own paragraph. After quarter-end, NVIDIA signed a guarantee of up to US$105 billion supporting SB Energy data-center sites whose capacity OpenAI will use. It phases in across nine sites as each becomes ready for service — the first expected in FY2029 — shrinks as the tenant pays, and can end early under specified credit conditions. The existing, smaller guarantee pool has US$0.712 billion of escrow behind it. The risk is what happens on failure: if a supported customer defaults, NVIDIA may inherit long leases, keep paying site costs, or make substantial payments, and getting money back through indemnities may be slow or incomplete.

Separately, NVIDIA talks about helping mobilize more than US$500 billion of third-party infrastructure capital. Today that rests on preliminary, nonbinding memoranda — outside investors still underwrite each project themselves, with NVIDIA possibly offering project-specific residual-value support. Treat it as a financing funnel, not funded capacity.

PLAIN ENGLISH

Think of NVIDIA as quietly adding a second job: part chip monopolist, part development bank for the AI build-out. The evidence: customers now take longer to pay (DSO — the average days to collect a sale — jumped from 45 to 60 in one quarter, and five customers owe roughly US$44bn between them); NVIDIA has signed US$366bn of scheduled commitments — mostly supply and capacity, plus cloud, leases, equity and capex — and US$56bn of additional agreements; and its maximum guarantees, at US$108.5bn, are 1.9× its own cash and bonds.

Three cautions on reading those giant numbers. They are gross ceilings, not expected losses — the loss probabilities are undisclosed. The filing provides no overlap reconciliation, so they cannot simply be added into one total. And the biggest guarantee doesn't even start until FY2029. The problem is narrower: management was directly asked what the cash schedule, return hurdle and loss tolerance are — and did not answer. Shareholders are underwriting a credit book with no published credit policy.

CAPITAL ALLOCATION · SBC · GOVERNANCE

Buybacks offset dilution; investments change the risk budget

Over the cycle NVIDIA gives capital back: from FY2022 through the first half of FY2027, buybacks and dividends totaled about US$141.7 billion, against roughly US$29.1 billion flowing in from employee stock plans and net new debt — about 4.9 dollars returned per dollar raised. The ratio does not erase the existing debt or the growing investment and commitment perimeter — and the mix is shifting: this half-year, more cash went into equity stakes in other companies (US$42.4bn) than into buybacks (US$39.0bn).

One weakness remains: the single-year executive metric adds SBC back, reducing discipline over the equity-pay cost itself.

Founder influence is economic, not majority control

Founder-CEO Jen-Hsun Huang holds 3.58% of the company per the March proxy (all directors and officers together: 3.94%), and part of his stake sits in foundation shares in which the proxy says he has no financial interest. The board had nine independent directors plus Huang at the proxy date, with an independent lead director; a July appointment later expanded it. So the founder leads by influence and track record, not by votes.

The big index managers' stakes carry mismatched dates: Vanguard's 7.31% is as of end-March 2026, while BlackRock's 7.43% rests on a December 2023 holding, split-adjusted — so the apparent ranking means little.

Governance watch items: board risk oversight explicitly covers business-model, supply-chain, investment and regulatory risks — yet NVIDIA discloses no Chief Compliance Officer even as export controls and customer financing grow more consequential. Related-person deals need governance-committee approval. The proxy also notes Huang's two adult children work at NVIDIA under standard compensation processes, neither reporting to him.

One relationship to watch rather than fear: the Huang Foundation agreed to buy CoreWeave GPU compute to donate to universities and nonprofits, while NVIDIA planned ordinary-course free engineering help for certain recipients. The proxy does not put a number on NVIDIA's service cost and does not treat the foundation's spending as NVIDIA revenue. It is a perception and conflict-monitoring item, not evidence of circular revenue.

Competition inquiries are broad; China is further advanced

Antitrust regulators in the EU, U.S., UK, China and South Korea have all asked NVIDIA for information — covering GPU sales, supply allocation, investments in AI model companies, roadmaps, and customer and supplier agreements. France is separately examining whether gaming and data-center GPUs are distinct markets.

China is furthest along: on 15 September 2025, its regulator preliminarily found that NVIDIA's export-compliant degraded products discriminated against Chinese customers and breached conditions of the Mellanox merger approval. Possible remedies range from fines to restrictions on networking products and operations. The filing discloses no maximum exposure or reserve for these matters; the only closure is a formal disposition — clean or otherwise.

A March 2026 court order certified a shareholder class action over 2017–2018 channel-inventory and crypto-demand statements; damages are unspecified and NVIDIA is defending the case. A governance reminder, not a quantified valuation deduction.

PLAIN ENGLISH

The shareholder-friendly facts: buybacks and dividends have returned about 4.9× what stock plans and new debt brought in; executive stock awards carry real profit and share-price hurdles; and the CEO's equity awards are performance-only. The watch-list: US$19.4bn of already-granted stock pay will hit expenses over the coming years; five antitrust authorities are asking questions, with China already at a preliminary adverse finding with no disclosed maximum penalty; and the company has no disclosed Chief Compliance Officer while its export-control and customer-financing exposures grow.

MARKET · FORECAST · VALUATION

The price discounts execution, not a demand collapse

Valuing NVIDIA means answering three questions in order: what does the market pay today, what is the chip business itself worth under different futures, and how much should be deducted for the financing promises documented above? This section walks through all three, then reads back what the current price implies.

Step 3 · The model: value the chip business, then charge for the promises

Vocabulary for the tables. Owner FCFF (free cash flow to the firm) is this report's strict cash measure: after-tax operating profit, plus depreciation, minus capital spending and minus the cash absorbed as working capital — the receivables and inventory growth documented earlier — with stock pay treated as a real expense (no add-back). A DCF (discounted cash flow) totals those future cash flows in today's money; the WACC (weighted average cost of capital) is the annual return investors demand — higher risk means a higher rate and a lower value — and terminal growth (g) is the assumed permanent growth after the forecast years. The capital-risk haircut is this model's signature deduction: an assumed loss rate applied to the scheduled commitments and guarantees, charged directly against value. NWC is net working capital; D&A is depreciation and amortization. A basis point (bp) is one hundredth of a percentage point — 350bp = 3.5%.

Step 4 · What the current price implicitly assumes

Run the Base model backwards, changing only next year's growth: the after-hours price implies FY28 revenue growth of about 46.457% — well below management's own preliminary outlook of 70%. In plain terms, the market is not paying for management's forecast. That is not automatic proof the stock is cheap: the discount may be the market's charge for guidance credibility, margin durability, the working-capital drag, possible commitment losses, or a lower long-run multiple. But it does mean a demand collapse is not what today's price assumes.

External cross-check: after the call, Goldman raised its twelve-month target to US$300 from US$285, keeping a 30× multiple on normalized EPS raised to US$10 from US$9.50. That target sits inside this report's Base interval — but it uses the same normalized-earnings logic, so it is agreement, not independent confirmation. Goldman's Jan-2029 forecast is more bullish than this report's Base on revenue, margin and EPS.
RISKS · MONITORING

The operating thesis is intact; the evidentiary burden is higher

Strongest evidence for

Data Center, cloud demand and architecture cadence remain exceptional. Rubin is already shipping, and the outlook excludes a China recovery.

Strongest evidence against

Part of future demand depends on NVIDIA investments, extended terms, cloud purchases, leases or guarantees whose returns and loss probabilities are undisclosed.

Alternative explanation

Goldman's post-call view is that clearer margin and commitment disclosure improves risk assessment. It does not resolve cash timing, contract overlap or loss probability.

Resolution path

Track sell-through, utilization, receivable aging, commitment reduction, realized guarantee cash, Rubin mix and normalized gross margin together.

FINAL JUDGMENT

Constructive, but no longer a pure platform multiple

NVIDIA still owns the strongest operating evidence in AI infrastructure. The new discount rate belongs to credit allocation, not to demand.

At the post-results price, the Base valuation spans US$254–321 per share — above the US$219.53 indication. The Bear range is US$106–148; the Bull methods converge near US$538–551. Upside outweighs downside across the scenarios, which supports a constructive long-term view. But this is not a low-risk earnings chase: only the lower end of the Base range offers a modest margin of safety, and the Bear case sits roughly half the current price away.

Stance: positive, with a higher risk classification than a pure chip-platform story. The thesis strengthens if the next quarter's guidance turns into real Rubin shipments, cash collection and stable receivable quality while the scheduled commitments shrink through use. It weakens if gross margin breaks the promised floor, or if the guarantees start converting into assumed leases and actual cash payments.

Strongest counterargument: management's preliminary FY2028 outlook is limited by supply, not demand — and the balance-sheet structures may end up as a profitable second business with few realized losses. Failure boundary: customer-financed demand, weaker cash conversion and lower margins persisting together would invalidate the premium platform valuation. Confidence is medium: the operating disclosures are strong, but customer-level utilization, returns and credit economics are absent.
