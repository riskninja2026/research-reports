THE THIRTY-SECOND VERSION

If you read only one screen, read this one

  • Who PDD is: the company behind Pinduoduo, China's discount-shopping app, and Temu, its international arm. It mostly does not sell goods itself — merchants do, and they pay PDD for advertising and per-order services. It trades on Nasdaq at US$87.07 per ADS (its U.S.-listed share unit — defined in the primer below).
  • The state of the business: still a profit machine. Q2 revenue was RMB112.4bn (+8.1%), operating profit RMB27.8bn at a 24.7% margin, and operating cash flow grew 18.6%. The balance sheet holds RMB456bn of cash and short-term investments — about US$67bn — with no reported borrowings.
  • The catch this quarter: net income still fell 11.6%. An unexplained RMB7.4bn "other loss" plus higher tax wiped out the operating improvement, and the company would not say what the loss was.
  • The trap for a new reader: PDD reports everything as one segment. You cannot see whether Temu makes or loses money; there are no user counts, no merchandise volume, no disclosure of the platform's cut of each sale. The stock looks cheap, but the cheapness cannot be verified.
  • The verdict: watchlist, not a clean buy. The models value the ADS at US$59–70 in the bear case and US$112–113 in the base case; the US$87.07 quote sits between them. A better entry needs either real disclosure or a mechanism that returns some of the cash pile to shareholders.
PRIMER · THREE MINUTES ON THE BUSINESS

PDD sells attention to merchants, not goods to shoppers

PDD runs a marketplace: outside merchants list and sell the goods, and PDD charges the merchants. That is why its accounting customers are merchants, not shoppers, and why its revenue is a fraction of the merchandise value flowing through the apps. The money arrives through two lines: online marketing services (merchants pay for visibility, like search ads) and transaction services (per-order commissions and fulfillment-related fees).

STEP 1
Shoppers come for price

Pinduoduo and Temu aggregate demand for inexpensive goods. Using the apps is free for consumers.

STEP 2
Merchants compete

16.8 million active merchants (2025) fight for placement in front of those shoppers.

STEP 3
PDD charges the merchants

Advertising for visibility, plus transaction and fulfillment fees on each order.

STEP 4
Profit piles up as cash

PDD owns little inventory or infrastructure itself, so profit converts to cash instead of warehouses.

Two structural facts matter before any table. First, the listing structure: what you buy on Nasdaq is an ADS — a receipt representing four Class A ordinary shares of a Cayman Islands parent, which controls the mainland-China operating companies through contracts (a VIE structure) rather than direct ownership. Second, the disclosure structure: Pinduoduo and Temu are reported together as a single e-commerce segment. The company discloses no split of revenue or profit between China and abroad, no GMV, no user counts and no take rate. Terms with a dotted underline are defined in the glossary near the end.

Industry background: Chinese e-commerce growth has matured, and PDD's international arm Temu — which grew by shipping cheap parcels directly from China — now faces tariffs and platform regulation in its biggest Western markets. PDD's answer is "localization": building local merchant networks and warehouses abroad. That transition is the central bet of this report, and it is happening inside the one segment you cannot see.

PLAIN ENGLISH

The five-year story: revenue multiplied nearly 4.6 times in four years — from RMB94bn to RMB432bn — and along the way PDD became the most profitable operator in its e-commerce peer group by operating margin, with returns on shareholders' equity above 40% at the 2024 peak. 2025 was the first down year: operating profit fell about 14% in the year the company stepped up spending on its transitions — though the single-segment accounts make it impossible to see exactly where the money went.

This quarter continues the slower era: growth of 8.1% against a four-year average of 46% means the hypergrowth phase is over. Profitability is still excellent — roughly a quarter of every revenue yuan becomes operating profit — and cash keeps piling up. Two gaps to note: the release does not disclose capital spending, so free cash flow for the quarter cannot be computed; and every "gross profit", "ROE" and "BVPS" figure here is analyst-derived from the filings, not a number the company itself reports.

The displayed marketing CAGR is not a pure advertising measure. Its recast 2021 base includes RMB7.246 billion of direct merchandise sales, which fell to RMB0.209 billion in 2022 before becoming immaterial.

Gross profit is revenue less cost of revenue; each margin divides the named profit by revenue. Four-year CAGR uses (2025 value / 2021 value)1/4 − 1 on unrounded inputs, and stream mix divides stream revenue by total revenue. Annual ROE divides attributable net income by average beginning-and-ending parent equity; LTM ROE uses June 2025 and June 2026 equity. BVPS per ADS equals period-end parent equity divided by ordinary shares, multiplied by four. Annual FCF proxy is operating cash flow less purchases of property, equipment, software and intangible assets; it is not a company KPI. LTM equals FY2025 plus H1 2026 less H1 2025. Low statistical beta does not make the operating model defensive

Statistical beta. Regressed weekly against the Nasdaq Composite over two years, PDD's beta is 0.58 with an R-squared of just 6.1%. The second number is the important one: the index explains almost none of PDD's price moves. PDD's price path is mostly unexplained by the index — and that is a statement about price behavior, not proof the business is defensive.

Operating cyclicality. The business itself still moves with retail demand and merchant ad budgets. Localization adds fulfillment and compliance costs; the debt-free balance sheet at least limits how much financing amplifies the cycle.

Competitive excess return. The breadth of the merchant base and the growth of transaction fees are genuine positives. But with users, GMV, retention, take rate and per-line margins all withheld, a durable competitive edge can be suggested — not proven.

PLAIN ENGLISH

PDD's two revenue lines are behaving very differently. Advertising grew only 3.5%, while per-order transaction services grew 13.3% and delivered 76.9% of the quarter's entire revenue increase. The growth is therefore coming from per-order fees rather than from merchants bidding more for ads — and what that shift means for profit cannot be checked from outside, because PDD does not disclose the profitability of either line.

And note how little you are allowed to see: merchant statistics are updated once a year, and there is no per-platform breakdown. The "beta" paragraph above says something simple — PDD's share price marches to its own drummer (policy headlines, disclosure events), not to the tech index — so index-level calm offers no shelter here.

Two European hits landed at once. First, from July 2026 to July 2028, every direct-to-consumer parcel under €150 pays a €3 customs duty for each distinct tariff category it contains. Second, the European Commission fined Temu €200 million for an inadequate systemic-risk assessment under the DSA. The cash fine is easily affordable; the remediation may be the real cost, because it can reach assortment, merchant screening, conversion and compliance spend.

Temu's original trick — shipping small parcels straight from Chinese factories to Western doorsteps, mostly duty-free — is being legislated away. PLAIN ENGLISH

Forward P/E is the price divided by next year's expected earnings — the lower it is, the less you pay per dollar of expected profit. PDD costs 7.8× expected earnings while every profitable peer costs 11× to 49×, even though PDD has the best operating margin in the group. Markets rarely leave that gap open by accident: the low multiple is the price of the missing disclosure, the regulatory attack on Temu, and a cash pile shareholders may never touch. The steps below try to turn that intuition into numbers.

Step 3 · The model: value the business, then add a haircut cash pile

Vocabulary for the tables: EBIT is operating profit. NOPAT is EBIT after tax, and FCFF (free cash flow to the firm) is the cash the business generates after tax and reinvestment. A DCF (discounted cash flow) totals those future cash flows in today's money; the required return is the annual return an investor demands for the risk (higher risk → higher rate → lower value), and the terminal value captures everything beyond the forecast years at a modest permanent growth rate. EV (enterprise value) is the market's price for the operating business after separating out net financial assets. NFA credit is the fraction of the financial-asset pool the scenario treats as truly belonging to shareholders.

STEP 3A
Value the operating business

Project three years of revenue growth and EBIT margin per scenario, convert to free cash flow, fade toward a terminal state, and discount everything back at the required return (a DCF).

STEP 3B
Add the cash pile — with a haircut

Step 4 · What the current price implicitly assumes

What the price embeds

With the base margin and asset-credit assumptions, the reverse DCF requires revenue to contract 2.72% annually through 2030. Under the bear asset credit, required growth is only 0.86%.

Alternative explanation

At mid-single-digit growth and the base asset credit, the current price implies a 15.69% EBIT margin. The discount can express structural cost, inaccessible cash, or both.

One more way to see the same tension: after the asset haircut, today's enterprise value equals just 4.94× the last twelve months' operating profit. Grant the business the base-case multiple instead, and the arithmetic forces the other side down — the market would then be crediting only 33% of the broad financial-asset proxy. Cheap business or cheap cash pile: the current price lets you claim one, not both in full.

THE BOTTOM LINE

A "reverse DCF" runs the machine backwards: instead of asking what PDD is worth, it asks what the US$87.07 price already assumes. The answer: if you trust the base-case margins and cash credit, today's price assumes revenue shrinks about 2.7% a year through 2030 — for a company still growing 8%. That is genuine pessimism embedded in the price. But the price is also a package deal: it grants the business only ~5× operating profit or it grants only a third of the cash pile — you cannot claim the stock is cheap on both counts at once. Against the bear value of $59–70, the risk-reward is positive but not lopsided, which is why the verdict is a watchlist, not a buy.

RISK VALIDATION · BULL / BEAR

The thesis resolves on disclosure, not on another cash record

Best case for the equity

Consolidated profit and cash are self-funding. Transaction services retain momentum, and localization could improve reliability while regulation raises barriers for weaker competitors.

Best case against it

One-segment reporting may conceal a transfer of domestic economics into low-return fulfillment, first-party inventory, merchant support and compliance. Retained cash has no return mechanism.

FINAL JUDGMENT

Watchlist, not a clean value call

The balance sheet offers real protection. But the investment case asks shareholders to fund several capital-intensive transitions at once — with no platform-level economics disclosed and no mechanism for cash to come back. The models say US$59–70 per ADS in the bear case and US$112–113 in the base case; the market quote sits between them, and where it lands mostly depends on how much of the asset pool is credited and how durable the operating margin proves. Attractive enough to watch closely; not transparent enough to hold as a high-conviction compounder.

Stance: wait for operating proof. A stronger entry requires either disclosure that localized Temu and first-party initiatives earn positive contribution returns, or a durable mechanism that converts retained financial assets into per-ADS value.

Strongest counterargument: the opaque accounts may be concealing a profitable global engine, and the cash pile lets management adapt without ever asking markets for money. Failure boundary: growth and margins repeatedly below the base case, together with continued silence on what the cash is for, would break the recovery case. Confidence is medium-low — the platform economics and the original-language call record simply are not available.

GLOSSARY · IN ORDER OF APPEARANCE

The terms you need for a platform company

TermPlain-English meaning
ADSAmerican Depositary Share — the Nasdaq-traded receipt for foreign shares. One PDD ADS represents four Class A ordinary shares of the Cayman parent.
MarketplaceA platform where outside merchants sell the goods. The platform earns fees, not the retail price — so its revenue is far smaller than the goods flowing through it.
First-party (1P)The opposite model: the platform itself owns and sells the inventory. PDD's new "first-party brands" push is a step in this direction.
Online marketing servicesPDD's advertising line: merchants pay for visibility and traffic.
Transaction servicesPDD's per-order line: commissions and fulfillment-related fees charged on each sale.
SegmentA business unit a company reports separately. PDD reports only ONE segment, so Pinduoduo and Temu economics cannot be separated by outsiders.
GMVGross merchandise value — total value of goods sold through a platform. PDD no longer discloses it.
Take ratePlatform revenue divided by GMV — the platform's cut of each transaction. Not computable for PDD without GMV.
VIEVariable interest entity — the contractual structure that lets an offshore listed parent consolidate a mainland-China business it cannot directly own.
OCFOperating cash flow — cash actually generated by running the business, before investment spending.
LTMLast twelve months — a rolling one-year total that smooths out seasonality.
ROEReturn on equity — net income divided by shareholders' equity; the yearly return earned on shareholders' capital.
BVPSBook value per share — net assets divided by the share count.
SBCShare-based compensation — paying employees in stock or options. An expense to shareholders through dilution even when no cash leaves.
EBITEarnings before interest and tax — operating profit; the profit of the business itself, ignoring the investment portfolio.
FCFFFree cash flow to the firm — cash the operating business produces after tax and reinvestment; the raw material of a DCF.
DCFDiscounted cash flow — a valuation that adds up forecast future cash flows in today's money, using a required return as the exchange rate between future and present.
Terminal valueThe DCF's estimate of all value beyond the explicit forecast years, assuming a modest permanent growth rate.
EV / EV-EBITEnterprise value — the price of the operating business after separating net financial assets. EV/EBIT says how many years of operating profit the market pays for it.
NFANet financial assets — the broad financial-asset pool (RMB547.953bn proxy here) valued separately from the operating business.
Forward P/EPrice divided by next year's expected earnings — how many dollars you pay per dollar of expected profit.
De minimisThe small-parcel duty-free customs exemption Temu's cross-border model relied on; now suspended in the U.S.
DSAThe EU's Digital Services Act — the platform-safety law under which Temu was fined €200m.
